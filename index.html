What each book spends its words on

One column per tradition, every passage of its scriptures one pixel, colored by what it does. A tradition holding several books stacks them down the column with a band between: the Quran above the six hadith collections, the Vinaya above the five nikayas. Narrative is left as ground, being the commonest kind in most of them. Below each map, the share carrying each kind — every bar on one scale, so a row compares the six straight across, and a passage can do more than one thing, so they do not add to a hundred. Hover a kind to see only that kind, or a pixel to read the passage and what it was labelled.

Provenance — a hadith's chain of narrators, the Thus have I heard opening a sutta — says how a passage reached you, not what it does, and nearly every hadith has one. It is excluded by default, and excluded rather than hidden: a passage that is nothing but a chain leaves the count and the map altogether.

Six traditions, opened at random

A column is a tradition, not a book. Islam has seven books on the shelf here and Judaism one, and each jump takes one of them down and opens it at a passage chosen at random — so the Islam column falls open sometimes on the Quran and sometimes on Bukhari. Whatever passage sits on a column's reading line keeps its ink; the rest of the page fades around it. Scroll a column to move its light, or open all six somewhere new. Drop to a single tradition and you can ask for one of its books by name.

The marks in the margin

Each passage is labelled by what it does. Five of those kinds are marked with a colored rule in a fixed slot beside the text — law, promise of reward, threat, violence, doctrine — and a passage often carries more than one: a command with a punishment attached gets two. The slot is fixed, so an indented lone rule is a threat and not a law whether or not you can tell the colors apart. The remaining two kinds — narrative and worship — are named in the column head for whichever passage is lit. Threat and violence are kept apart on purpose: a threat warns the hearer what is coming for them, while violence is harm described, whoever it falls on. Being the victim is not being threatened. The labels are a model's reading of the form, not anyone's doctrine, and they are wrong often enough to be worth checking against the passage beside them.
